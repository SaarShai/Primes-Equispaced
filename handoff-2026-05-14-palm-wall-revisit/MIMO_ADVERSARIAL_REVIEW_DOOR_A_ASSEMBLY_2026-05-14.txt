**Review of Conditional Proof Assembly: AllZeroShiftedNeg_2(E)**

**1. Conductor-Flip Rule and Uniformity of Substitution (Question 1)**

The substitution $k \to 2k$ is the correct formal correspondence between the Riemann zeta function archimedean scale ($\log T$) and the GL(2)/elliptic curve scale ($\log C_E(t) \sim 2 \log T$). However, the claim that this substitution is uniform "everywhere in BFMT Section 5" requires careful verification of the interaction between the conductor normalization and the specific terms in the Weil explicit formula.

In **BFMT Section 5**, the archimedean contribution enters via the parameter $\beta_0$ in the majorant construction (specifically in the definition of the weight function $W$ and the subsequent majorant $M_{\beta_0}$).

*   **Equation (5.6):** The majorant is defined as $M_{\beta_0}(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}[W_{\beta_0}]$. The width of the Gaussian $W_{\beta_0}$ is inversely proportional to $\beta_0$. In the zeta case, $\beta_0 \sim (1+\delta)k \log T$. Under the conductor flip, we substitute $\log T \to 2 \log T$.
*   **Equation (5.13) and (5.17):** The exponent in (5.17) arises from integrating $M_{\beta_0}(t)$ against the test function. The term $(1+\delta)k \log T$ appears in the denominator of the ratio defining the exponent.
    *   **Uniformity Check:** The substitution $k \to 2k$ applies to the *frequency* parameter in the Fourier transform (which scales the zero density), while the conductor $C_E$ scales the *time* domain.
    *   In (5.17), the inner ratio is $\frac{2k(2k - A)}{2k - A + B}$. The claim substitutes $k \to 2k$, yielding $\frac{2(2k)(4k - A)}{2(2k) - A + B}$. This step assumes that the scaling of the test function width (via $\beta_0$) is linear in $k$ and $\log T$.
    *   **Potential Non-Uniformity:** In **BFMT (5.13)**, the constant $A = \frac{a(2d-1)}{r}$ depends on the Selberg eigenvalue $\lambda$ (via $a, r, d$). The claim notes $A, B = 1 + O(k \epsilon)$. However, under the conductor flip, the "critical strip" width for GL(2) is effectively doubled relative to the zeta normalization. If the parameters $a, r, d$ (which define the test function class) are held fixed while $k$ is doubled, the condition $A \approx 1$ might not hold identically for the GL(2) majorant without re-optimizing the test function parameters specifically for the $2 \log T$ scale.
    *   **Conclusion:** The substitution $k \to 2k$ is valid, but one must verify that the "small block" parameters $A$ and $B$ in (5.17) do not acquire additional dependencies on $T$ due to the conductor flip. The claim asserts $A, B = 1 + O(k \epsilon)$, which is sufficient if the error term absorbs the conductor scaling.

**2. Branch Routing and Hidden Small-Block Dependence (Question 2)**

The branch routing relies on the condition $2k(1+\epsilon) > 1$ for zeta and $4k(1+\epsilon) > 1$ for GL(2).

*   **BFMT (5.17) Context:** The second branch of (5.17) is active when the "excess" parameter $\xi = 2k(1+\epsilon) - 1$ is positive but small.
*   **Hidden Small-Block Dependence:** The claim correctly identifies that at $q=2$ (so $k=1$), the condition $4k(1+\epsilon) > 1$ holds. However, **BFMT Section 5** contains a "Wave 5" analysis (near eq. 5.11-5.12) involving the small-block contribution.
    *   The "small block" refers to the region near the origin in the zero density integral. The claim states the small-block sign condition is bypassed because the $q=2$ target uses the second branch directly.
    *   **Critique:** While the second branch is active, the *derivation* of (5.17) relies on approximating the zero density $N(\sigma, T)$ via a majorant integral. This integral involves a sum over "blocks" of zeros. The "Wave 5" analysis (BFMT Section 5.2) handles the case where $A > 4k$ (the "strong" target).
    *   For the "weak" target $T^{5/2+\epsilon}$, the condition $A \approx 1$ is used. However, the transition between the "small block" (near the critical line) and the "large block" (archimedean scaling) is governed by the parameter $\beta_0$. If the conductor flip shifts the effective width of the small block relative to the large block, the bookkeeping in **BFMT (5.11)-(5.13)** might introduce a boundary term.
    *   **Verdict:** The branch routing is correct *algebraically*, but the analytic justification in BFMT Section 5 assumes the zeta normalization for the block partitioning. One must confirm that the partitioning of the test function support (implicit in the majorant construction) remains valid when $\log T \to 2 \log T$. If the partitioning is uniform in $\log T$, the claim holds.

**3. Props 2.5/2.6/2.7 Lift to $k=1$ (Question 3)**

The transcription from $k=1/2$ (standard zero-density) to $k=1$ (shifted point) involves the zero-sampling lemma.

*   **BFMT Props 2.5, 2.6, 2.7:** These propositions bound the majorant sum $\sum_\rho M(\rho)$.
*   **Zero-Sampling Lemma (BFMT Lemma 3.1):** The claim correctly notes that the extra factor $(\log T)^2 \exp(O_E(s_0))$ is $k$-independent.
*   **The $k^2$ Factor:**
    *   Props 2.6 and 2.7 contain a factor $k^2 b(\Delta_j)^2 (\dots)^{2\eta(\Delta_j)}$.
    *   At $k=1/2$, $k^2 = 1/4$.
    *   At $k=1$, $k^2 = 1$.
    *   The claim states this is a "factor-of-4 numerical bump absorbed in $T^\epsilon$." This is valid, as $4 = T^{o(1)}$.
*   **Support Conditions:**
    *   The support of the majorant $M_{\beta_0}(t)$ is determined by $\beta_0$.
    *   Under the conductor flip, $\beta_0 \to \beta_0/2$ (since $\log T \to 2 \log T$).
    *   The zero-sampling lemma requires the support of the test function to be sufficiently small. The claim asserts products are preserved. Specifically, if the support width scales as $1/\beta_0$, and $\beta_0$ halves, the support doubles. However, the *density* of zeros scales with $T$. The condition $|\rho| \approx T$ and the support width $O(1/\beta_0)$ must be compatible.
    *   **Critique:** The support condition in the zero-sampling lemma (BFMT Lemma 3.1) is sensitive to the spacing of zeros. While the $k$-independence of the lemma is correct, the *application* at $k=1$ requires the test function to be sufficiently localized relative to the mean spacing of zeros ($1/\log T$). Since $\beta_0 \sim (1+\delta)k \log T$, doubling $k$ doubles the localization frequency, which is consistent with the GL(2) scaling.
    *   **Verdict:** The lift arguments are valid. The $k^2$ factor is harmless, and the support conditions are preserved because the localization $\beta_0$ scales linearly with $k$ and $\log T$.

**4. Multiplicity Extension (Question 4)**

The extension to multiple zeros ($m_\rho > 1$) is handled via the Taylor expansion of $L_E^*(s)$.

*   **RvM Lemma (Rudnick-Venkatesh Model):** The claim cites RvM to bound $m_\rho = O_E(\log T)$.
*   **Weighting:** The contribution is weighted by $m_\rho (\log T)^{2m_\rho} |L_E^{(m_\rho)}(\rho)/m!|^{-2}$.
*   **BFMT Section 5 Assumptions:** The majorant construction $M_{\beta_0}(t)$ in BFMT Section 5 is derived for simple zeros (the density $N(\sigma, T)$ counts simple zeros).
    *   **Implicit Assumption:** The derivation of (5.17) uses the Weil explicit formula, which sums over zeros with multiplicity. The majorant $M_{\beta_0}$ is constructed to dominate the counting function $N(\sigma, T)$.
    *   **Critique:** If zeros have multiplicity $m > 1$, the density $N(\sigma, T)$ counts them $m$ times. The majorant $M_{\beta_0}$ must dominate this weighted count. The claim correctly notes that the per-zero factor is $T^{o(1)}$.
    *   However, the *derivation* of the exponent in (5.17) assumes a distribution of zeros with mean spacing $1/\log T$. High multiplicity locally alters this spacing. The RvM bound $m_\rho = O(\log T)$ implies that the "effective spacing" near a multiple zero could be as small as $1/(m_\rho \log T)$.
    *   **Conclusion:** The total multiple-zero contribution is $T^{1+o(1)}$, which is negligible compared to $T^{5/2}$. The BFMT Section 5 estimates do not *assume* simple zeros explicitly; they bound the counting function. Since the counting function includes multiplicity, and the bound on multiplicity is weak ($O(\log T)$), the exponent $5/2$ is preserved. No cross-effect changes the exponent.
